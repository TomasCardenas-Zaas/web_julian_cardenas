01 — AN OPEN QUESTION

I   /   A vocabulary

One word.
Several meanings.

To ask whether truth is relative, it helps to separate what we can observe from what we make of it. A fact describes what happened; an interpretation gives it meaning. Both matter, but they answer different questions. By noticing where they meet—and where they diverge—we can better understand why accounts differ, and how evidence might bring us closer to a shared view.

Objective truth

A claim about reality that does not depend on any one person believing it. Whether a particular claim is true still has to be investigated; objectivity is an aim, not a guarantee that we have perfect access to the facts.

Subjective truth

What an experience feels like from the inside: real to the person living it, shaped by their point of view. Two people may describe the same encounter differently without either inventing how it felt. Yet when their accounts make incompatible claims about an observable detail, both cannot be factually accurate in the same respect. A sincere experience deserves to be heard; it does not, by itself, settle what happened.

Perspective

The position—personal, historical, or cultural—from which something is seen. Perspective can reveal details another observer misses, while also leaving other parts of the event out of view.

Evidence

What can support or challenge a claim: records, observations, and testimony, each open to scrutiny. Evidence does not interpret itself; we compare sources, ask how they were gathered, and consider what might count against our preferred explanation.

Belief

A claim we accept as true. Belief may follow evidence, but it can also precede it. Holding a belief makes it meaningful to its holder, not automatically true for everyone; its reasons remain open to question.

Bias & ideology

Patterns of expectation and systems of ideas that influence which evidence we notice, trust, or overlook. They shape interpretation across societies as well as individuals. Recognizing a bias does not, by itself, prove a claim false—but it can prompt us to examine our standards more carefully.

A tension worth keeping

When experiences
conflict.

Two people can sincerely describe the same encounter in opposite ways: one felt heard; the other felt dismissed. Those experiences can both be true as accounts of how each person felt.

But if they disagree about an observable detail—what was said, or when someone left—the accounts cannot both be accurate in the same respect. Sincerity does not settle the fact. Evidence can help us examine it, without making either person’s experience disappear.

II   /   The central question

Can there be
more than one truth?

One event can leave behind several honest accounts because no observer sees everything. Different accounts may add context and describe genuinely different experiences. But when they make incompatible claims about the same observable detail, they cannot all be factually correct in the same way. Multiple interpretations do not require multiple versions of the event itself.

IV   /   A provisional conclusion

What remains
between us.

The facts may be shared. Their meaning may not be.

Truth asks us to hold two ideas together: reality is not simply remade by each person’s perspective, yet our experiences shape how we understand it. Evidence helps us test claims about what happened; listening helps us see what those events meant to different people.

One delay is measured in minutes; it is remembered as a missed chance by one person and a gift of time by another. Neither response changes the timetable. Both help describe what the event became in a human life.

Perhaps meaning begins where fact meets experience: in what we notice, what we carry with us, and what the moment asks of us. But perspective has limits. A feeling can tell us what an event meant without, on its own, proving every detail of what occurred.

To listen is not necessarily to agree. It may be a way to see more of the same reality—to test our account against another, and to remain open to what neither account includes.

In brief

Truth is not simply whatever each person believes: claims about events can be tested against evidence. Yet perspective and experience shape how those events are understood, so honest accounts may differ in meaning even when facts remain shared. Distinguishing fact, interpretation, and belief—and examining our biases—helps us question what we think we know. Listening across perspectives can deepen understanding, while evidence keeps our accounts answerable to reality.

An unfinished thought

If two people see the same reality differently, are they seeing two truths?
